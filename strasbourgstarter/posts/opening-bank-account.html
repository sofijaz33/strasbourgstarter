How to Open a Bank Account in France as a Foreigner (Without Losing Your Mind)

July 2025 · 10 min read · Updated for 2025

Congratulations — you've made it to Strasbourg. The cathedral is breathtaking, the tarte flambée is better than you imagined, and you now need to open a bank account. Simple, right?

Wrong. Welcome to the French administrative catch-22 that has broken the spirit of thousands of perfectly capable adults before you. Here's how it goes: the rental agency won't give you a flat without a French phone number so they can contact you. The phone operator won't give you a contract without a French address. The bank won't open an account without proof of address. And you can't easily get proof of address without… a flat. Meanwhile you're sitting in an Airbnb watching your savings evaporate at €90 a night wondering why you didn't just stay home.

Deep breath. This guide will walk you through exactly how to escape the loop — step by step, in the right order, with no panic required.

⚡ Pro tip — read this first

The secret to breaking the cycle is to tackle things in a very specific order, and to use temporary solutions that unlock the next step — rather than trying to get everything perfectly set up at once. Think of it like a video game: you need the small key before you can open the big door.

Also: ask a colleague or friend if you can temporarily register at their address. It sounds awkward to ask, but every expat who has been in Strasbourg for more than six months has done exactly this for someone else. It's basically a Strasbourg rite of passage. Ask them to put your name on their mailbox — yes, literally — because some documents (your bank card, your social security number) will be physically posted to that address.

Step 1 — Get a French phone number on Day 1

Step 01

Buy a prepaid SIM card — no address needed

Your very first task, ideally before you've even unpacked your suitcase, is to get a French phone number. Not a contract — just a prepaid SIM (carte SIM prépayée). You can buy one at:

  • Any tabac (tobacconist shop — look for the red diamond sign)
  • Carrefour, Leclerc, or Auchan supermarkets
  • The Strasbourg-Ville train station (multiple phone shops)
  • Orange, SFR, or Bouygues stores in the city centre

Prepaid SIMs from Lebara, Lycamobile, or La Poste Mobile are your best bet — cheap (from €5–10), no contract, no French bank account required, and you'll have a real French +33 number within minutes. You'll need your passport or ID card to register it — that's all.

This number is what you'll put on every single form you fill in for the next three months. Guard it with your life.

Reality check: Yes, you'll have a prepaid SIM with 5GB of data when you're used to unlimited everything. Yes, it will feel like 2009. Yes, you'll survive. Upgrade to a proper plan (Free Mobile at €8–12/month is excellent) once you have a French bank account. One battle at a time.

Step 2 — Sort a temporary address

Step 02

Use a friend, colleague, or your employer's HR department

If you don't yet have your own flat, you need a French address to use on documents. Here are your options, in order of ease:

  • Ask a colleague or friend if you can use their address temporarily. Tell them you'll need your name on their mailbox — important, because bank cards and official documents are sent by post and require your name to be on it. Most people will say yes without hesitation.
  • Ask your employer's HR team for a attestation d'hébergement — a letter confirming you are being hosted. Many international employers in Strasbourg (especially the EU institutions) are very familiar with this situation.
  • Use your Airbnb or hotel address as a last resort for online bank sign-ups — some accept this for the first 30 days while your file is being processed.
⚠️ One thing to know about French mail: Every first Wednesday of the month, sirens go off across all of France at noon as a national alert test (Des sirènes retentissent partout en France chaque premier mercredi du mois). It lasts about a minute and a half. Do not panic. Do not look for shelter. Just nod knowingly, because you now live here. We mention this because the first time it happens you will think something has gone catastrophically wrong — and you'll be standing in the post office trying to collect your bank card.

Step 3 — Open Wise or Revolut immediately

Step 03

Your financial lifeline while everything else gets sorted

Wise and Revolut are not French banks, but they give you a working Euro account with a French IBAN (starting with FR76) within 24–48 hours. You can sign up entirely on your phone, using your home country address if needed.

  • Wise is the most reliable for receiving international transfers and salary payments. Genuinely excellent exchange rates.
  • Revolut is faster to set up and has a French branch — so the IBAN actually works for most French services.

Caveats: some French employers and the CAF housing benefit office will not accept a Wise or Revolut IBAN for salary payments or benefit disbursements. Use these as a bridge, not a permanent solution. But they'll keep you going while you sort the real thing.

Step 4 — Get a real French IBAN with Nickel or N26

Step 04

The easiest "real" French bank account to open

Nickel is your secret weapon. It's technically a payment account (not a full bank), available at most tabac shops across Strasbourg. You walk in, show your passport, pay €20/year, and walk out with a real French RIB (relevé d'identité bancaire) and a Mastercard. No proof of address required. No credit check. No appointment.

A French RIB is the document that unlocks almost everything: rental contracts, employer salary payments, CAF registration. Once you have a RIB, the chicken-and-egg problem is officially solved.

N26 is the alternative if you prefer a fully digital setup — you can open it on your phone in about 10 minutes with just your passport and a selfie video. It gives you a French IBAN and is accepted by most employers.

  • Nickel: walk into any tabac, €20/year, instant RIB ✅
  • N26: fully online, free basic plan, French IBAN ✅
  • Bunq: another option, no French address required, works for EU residents ✅
Honest moment: The Nickel account has limitations — no cheque book, no overdraft, and your card can occasionally fail at unmanned toll booths on the motorway to Colmar. But at this stage of your Strasbourg adventure, you will not care. You will have a French RIB and you will feel like you have personally defeated the entire French bureaucratic system. You have not — but enjoy the feeling while it lasts.

Step 5 — Open a proper French bank account

Step 05

Once you have an address — go online first

Now that you have a flat, a French phone number, a temporary RIB, and proof of address, you can open a proper bank account. For most newcomers to Strasbourg, the best options are:

  • Boursorama Banque — France's largest online bank, no monthly fees, excellent app, accepts foreigners. Apply online, card arrives in about a week. The gold standard choice for expats.
  • Hello Bank (BNP Paribas digital) — Also free, good app, and backed by one of France's largest banks if you ever need a physical branch.
  • Crédit Mutuel — The most foreigner-friendly traditional bank in Alsace specifically. Some branches in Strasbourg have English-speaking staff. Good if you need face-to-face support.
  • La Banque Postale — At any post office. Slower, a bit clunky, but legally required to accept you (see the droit au compte section below).

Documents you'll typically need:

  • Valid passport or EU ID card
  • Proof of French address (rental contract, EDF electricity bill, or landlord's attestation)
  • Proof of income or situation (work contract, payslip, student enrolment, or self-employment certificate)
  • Your existing French RIB (from Nickel or N26) — some banks ask for this
  • For non-EU citizens: your visa or residence permit (titre de séjour)

What if a bank refuses you? The droit au compte

French law gives every legal resident the right to a basic bank account — this is called the droit au compte (right to an account). If a bank refuses to open an account for you, here is what you do:

  1. Get a written refusal from the bank (ask for it — they must provide one)
  2. Go to the Banque de France website or a branch and submit a droit au compte request
  3. The Banque de France will designate a bank (usually La Banque Postale) to open an account for you within 3 business days

It's not glamorous and you won't get a cheque book, but you will get a working French bank account and a RIB. No one can legally stop you at that point.

Will anyone at the bank speak English?

Honestly? At traditional French bank branches in Strasbourg — probably not, or at least not comfortably. Strasbourg is closer to Germany than to Paris in many ways, and you are more likely to find a teller who speaks German than one who speaks fluent English. This is not rudeness — it's just France.

A few practical strategies:

  • Crédit Mutuel branches in the centre of Strasbourg have the best track record for English-speaking advisors — call ahead to ask before making an appointment.
  • Online banks (Boursorama, Hello Bank, N26) handle everything via app and email — so the language barrier is largely a non-issue.
  • Bring a French-speaking colleague if you need to visit a branch. Buy them a coffee after. They deserve it.
  • Google Translate's camera mode works surprisingly well for French bank documents in a pinch.
  • The EU institutions often have internal guides or HR contacts who can help navigate banking — ask your onboarding team.
⚡ Final pro tip

Once you have a French bank account and a stable address, set up a Boursorama account as your main account — it's free, the app is excellent, and it handles everything including CAF payments and salary deposits. Keep Nickel or N26 as a backup card. Keep Wise for international transfers. You'll thank yourself in six months.

And when someone you know moves to Strasbourg after you? Let them use your address. Put their name on the mailbox. Pay it forward. It's the Strasbourg way.

The honest summary: The French banking system in 2025 is a bit like the tram network in Strasbourg — elegant, reliable, and deeply logical once you understand it, but completely baffling when you first arrive and you're standing at the wrong stop going the wrong direction. Give it three months. You'll be explaining the whole thing to the next bewildered newcomer before you know it.
