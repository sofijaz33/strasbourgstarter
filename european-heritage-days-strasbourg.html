European Heritage Days: The One Weekend a Year You Can Walk Into Buildings You'll Never See Again

Published September 2026 · 6 min read

By Sofija Zarkovic, founder of Strasbourg Starter

Twice a year Strasbourg quietly does something most newcomers never hear about until it's already over: it opens the doors of buildings that are normally locked, badge-only, or simply off-limits to the public — and lets anyone walk in, for free. This year it falls on Saturday 19 and Sunday 20 September. If you've been meaning to see the inside of somewhere in this city you only ever look at from the outside, this is the weekend.

What Journées du Patrimoine actually is

European Heritage Days happen every year on the third weekend of September, all across France and much of Europe. For two days, sites that are usually private, badge-access-only, or simply never open to visitors — state apartments, archives, institutional buildings, even some private courtyards — throw their doors open, free of charge. This year's national theme is heritage under threat, which in Strasbourg mostly translates into extra guided tours and behind-the-scenes access at sites tied to conservation work.

For a city built around EU institutions as much as medieval architecture, that combination is unusually good here: you get the cathedral and the half-timbered quarters everyone already knows, but you also get a once-a-year look inside the buildings that define the working half of Strasbourg.

The European Parliament, open to the public

On Sunday, 10am–4pm, the European Parliament opens its galleries and the hemicycle itself to anyone who walks up — no badge, no employer, no appointment needed. Entry is free, but it isn't anonymous: bring a valid ID, since checks are mandatory at the entrance.

This year there's an added reason to go even if you've already seen the building from a work pass: a special focus on the architecture of the Louise Weiss building, plus guided tours you can book in advance through the Parliament's own booking page.

Work at one of the institutions? This is the one weekend you can bring family or friends who don't have access badges and actually show them where you spend your day — most people never get the chance.

The Palais du Rhin — book early or queue

Less famous than the Parliament but arguably harder to get into any other day of the year: the Palais du Rhin, the former imperial residence on Place de la République, opens its state apartments for guided tours on Saturday only, 10am–4pm. Reservation is required and capacity is genuinely limited — previous years have seen 45-minute-to-1-hour queues form for people without a booking, so arriving before 9:45am is the realistic way in if you didn't reserve ahead.

The rest of the weekend: cathedral, Palais Rohan, and beyond

Beyond the two institutional headliners, the weekend is dense with things that are normally either paid or simply closed:

  • Cathédrale Notre-Dame — free both days, 9am–5:30pm, with reserved access to the panoramic platform (limited slots, so book if you want the view).
  • Palais Rohan — all three museums (Fine Arts, Decorative Arts, Archaeology) free for the weekend, opening at 10am.
  • Musée Alsacien — reconstructed 17th-century Alsatian interiors, 10am–6pm.
  • Gare centrale, the Préfecture du Bas-Rhin, and the departmental Archives — all open spaces that are simply never accessible the rest of the year.

The full, continuously-updated list of participating sites — with exact hours and which ones need booking — sits on the national Journées du Patrimoine programme, which is worth checking again the week of the event since a handful of venues confirm their hours late.

How to actually plan the two days

With so much open at once, the sites worth reserving ahead are the ones that cap numbers — the Palais du Rhin and the cathedral platform, mainly. Everything else you can genuinely just turn up to. Most of the sites cluster around two areas: the historic centre (cathedral, Palais Rohan, Musée Alsacien) and the European quarter (Parliament, Council of Europe-adjacent buildings) — worth splitting across the two days rather than trying to cross the city repeatedly. If you haven't spent much time exploring past your own quartier yet, this weekend is a good excuse to see parts of the city you'd otherwise walk past.

The short version

Book the Palais du Rhin and the cathedral platform in advance if you want either — both cap numbers. Bring ID for the European Parliament on Sunday. Everything else, just show up. And if you work at an institution, this is your one chance all year to actually bring someone along.
