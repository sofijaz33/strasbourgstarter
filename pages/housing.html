Housing in Strasbourg

Finding a flat in Strasbourg as a foreigner is entirely possible — but it helps enormously to know the rules of the game before you start. This guide covers everything: where to search, how to build a winning dossier, what to do if you have no French guarantor, and how to set up your electricity, gas and internet once you have the keys.

The Strasbourg rental market is competitive but not brutal — unlike Paris. Studios and one-bedroom flats in desirable areas like Neudorf or the city centre get snapped up within days, but there is a steady flow of new listings. The key is to be ready: have your dossier prepared before you start viewing, not after. Landlords here do not wait for you to get organised.

⚡ Before you start searching

Build your rental dossier first, then start viewing. A landlord in Strasbourg who receives a complete, well-presented dossier the same day as the viewing will almost always favour you over someone who says "I'll send the documents tomorrow." Use DossierFacile ↗ — it's a free government service that organises your documents into a standardised, verified digital dossier. Landlords trust it, and it makes you look serious.

Where to search — websites and agencies

The French rental market is split between private landlords listing directly online and professional agencies. Both have merits — agencies offer more security and English support in some cases, while private landlords are often more flexible on the dossier requirements. Start with the websites, then contact agencies if you're struggling.

What to expect to pay in 2026

Strasbourg is affordable by European capital standards — significantly cheaper than Paris, Lyon or Bordeaux. Rough monthly rent ranges for unfurnished properties:

  • Studio (20–30m²) — €450–650/month in most neighbourhoods, up to €750 in the city centre
  • 1-bedroom (35–50m²) — €600–850/month
  • 2-bedroom (55–75m²) — €800–1,100/month
  • 3-bedroom (80m²+) — €1,000–1,500/month

Furnished flats (meublé) cost roughly 10–20% more but come with appliances, furniture and often internet included. For a soft landing, a furnished flat for your first 3–6 months makes a lot of sense — you can search for something better once you know the city.

Add utilities on top: electricity and gas typically run €40–100/month depending on flat size and season, internet around €25–35/month.

Building your dossier de location

Your dossier de location is your rental application package. In Strasbourg's market, landlords often receive multiple applications for the same flat — a complete, well-organised dossier is what separates you from the pile. Use DossierFacile ↗ to build yours — it's free, government-backed, and landlords recognise the verified badge.

What goes in your dossier

  • Valid passport or EU ID card
  • Current visa or titre de séjour (non-EU citizens)
  • Proof of current address (even temporary — Airbnb booking confirmation works initially)
  • Work contract (contrat de travail) — a CDI (permanent contract) is gold; CDD (fixed-term) is fine
  • Last 3 payslips (bulletins de salaire)
  • Last 3 bank statements (relevés de compte)
  • Last tax return (avis d'imposition) — if you don't have a French one yet, use your home country equivalent
  • Your guarantor's equivalent documents (see below)
⚠️ The income rule: French landlords typically require that your monthly income is at least 3 times the monthly rent. So for a flat at €700/month, you need to show income of at least €2,100/month. If you don't meet this threshold, a guarantor becomes essential — see the next section.
On tax returns: The French tax return (avis d'imposition) covers the previous year. If you've just arrived in France, you obviously don't have one. Simply include a note explaining this with your home country equivalent, and add a letter from your employer confirming your position and salary. Most reasonable landlords understand — you just need to show you've thought about it.

The guarantor problem — and how to solve it

A garant (guarantor) is someone who legally agrees to pay your rent if you can't. French landlords love them and many will not rent to you without one. The classic requirement is a French resident with income of at least 3x your rent — which is exactly what most newcomers don't have.

Here are your realistic options, in order of ease:

  • Visale — the best option if you're eligible. It's a completely free guarantor service run by Action Logement, a French government-backed organisation. Visale covers your rent if you default, at zero cost to you. Eligibility: under 30 years old regardless of situation, or any age if you're a new employee (started work in the last 6 months). Check eligibility and apply at visale.fr ↗
  • Garantme — a paid private guarantor service (roughly 3.5% of annual rent). Accepts foreigners, processes applications quickly, and is widely recognised by Strasbourg landlords. Worth it if you don't qualify for Visale. garantme.fr ↗
  • SmartGarant — another paid alternative, similar to Garantme. smartgarant.fr ↗
  • Your employer — some large employers in Strasbourg (especially EU institutions and universities) will act as guarantor for new employees. Ask your HR team before you assume the answer is no.
  • A friend or colleague in France — if someone you know in France has a stable income of 3x your rent and is willing to sign, this works perfectly. The conversation is awkward to start but most people in Strasbourg's expat community understand immediately.
⚡ Pro tip

Apply for your Visale certificate before you start visiting flats — it takes 24–48 hours to arrive and having it in your dossier from the first visit is a significant advantage. Landlords see it as equivalent to a solid French guarantor.

Understanding your lease

French rental leases are standardised and heavily regulated in favour of tenants — which is reassuring once you understand the system. A few key things to check before you sign:

  • Notice period: For unfurnished flats, the standard notice period to leave is 3 months. In certain zones (Strasbourg qualifies as a zone tendue — high-demand area), this can be reduced to 1 month. Check your contract.
  • Security deposit (dépôt de garantie): Maximum 1 month's rent for unfurnished flats, 2 months for furnished. Must be returned within 1 month of your departure if no damage, or 2 months if deductions are made.
  • Charges: Check whether quoted rent is charges comprises (CC — includes utilities in the charges) or hors charges (HC — utilities billed separately). Always ask what the monthly charges (charges mensuelles) actually cover.
  • Inventory (état des lieux): Done on arrival and departure. Be meticulous — photograph everything, note every scratch. Disputes about the state of the flat are the most common reason landlords withhold deposits.
  • Home insurance (assurance habitation): Mandatory in France as a tenant. You must provide proof to your landlord before moving in. Budget around €8–20/month. Luko ↗ and MAIF ↗ are popular with expats.

CAF housing benefit — APL

The Aide Personnalisée au Logement (APL) is a monthly housing benefit paid directly into your bank account by the CAF (French family benefits office). Depending on your income and rent amount, it can be anywhere from €50 to €300+ per month — genuinely significant money.

You can apply as soon as you have a signed lease and a French bank account. The application is online at caf.fr ↗ — it's in French but manageable with a translation tool. You'll need:

  • Your signed lease
  • Your French bank account details (RIB)
  • Your social security number — if you don't have one yet, apply anyway and add it later
  • Proof of your income from the previous year
⚡ Don't delay this

Apply for APL the day you have your signed lease. Payments start from the month after application — every month you wait is money you don't get back. The whole process takes about 3–4 weeks from application to first payment.

Setting up electricity

As a tenant in France, setting up electricity in your name is your responsibility from day one of your lease. The good news: it can be done entirely online or by phone, and takes effect within a few days.

France's electricity market is deregulated — you can choose your supplier freely. The physical network (cables, meters, outage response) is always managed by Enedis regardless of your supplier. To open an account you'll need:

  • Your new address
  • Your PDL number (Point de Livraison) — the 14-digit meter reference, found on any previous bill or on the meter itself. Your landlord or agency should provide this.
  • The name of the previous occupant
  • Your French IBAN (RIB) for direct debit

Setting up gas

Not all flats in Strasbourg have mains gas (gaz de ville) — many modern buildings use electric heating only. Check your lease or ask your landlord before assuming. If your flat does have gas, you'll need a separate contract from your electricity provider.

The historic gas supplier is Engie (formerly GDF Suez). You'll need the same documents as for electricity, plus your PCE number (Point de Comptage et d'Estimation — the gas meter equivalent of the PDL). The process is identical: sign up online, give them your IBAN, and the account is activated within a few days.

The regulated gas tariff was abolished in France in 2023 — all contracts are now market rates, so it genuinely pays to compare. Selectra ↗ has English-speaking advisors who compare providers for free and open the contract in your name.

⚡ Internet timeline tip

Order internet on the day you sign your lease — installation appointments can take 2–3 weeks to schedule. In the meantime, your mobile data plan will be your lifeline. This is another reason to get a proper French SIM (rather than relying on roaming) as one of your very first steps.

Also: most French internet contracts have a 12-month commitment. If you're not sure how long you'll stay, look for a sans engagement (no-commitment) option — Free and SFR both offer these at a small premium.

The internet installation appointment: You will be given a 4-hour window. The technician will arrive either in the first 10 minutes or the last 10 minutes — never in the middle. You will rearrange your entire day. This is universal across all four providers and has been since the dawn of French internet. Budget the whole afternoon.
