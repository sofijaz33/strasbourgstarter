Whether you're joining an EU institution, a multinational, a university, or job hunting from scratch, this guide explains how it all works. Strasbourg has the second largest diplomatic presence in France after Paris. It is home to the European Parliament, the Council of Europe, the European Court of Human Rights, and dozens of international organisations. Alongside these institutions, the city has a strong university sector, a thriving biotech and pharmaceutical industry, and a growing tech scene. The job market here is more international than almost anywhere else in France — and being multilingual is genuinely valued. Job hunting in Strasbourg Finding a job in Strasbourg as a foreigner is easier than in most French cities, partly because the international community is large and partly because many employers are actively accustomed to multilingual candidates. That said, French language skills matter more outside the institutions — most private sector jobs expect at least B2 level French. Where to look LinkedIn — the most effective platform for international and institution roles in Strasbourg. Make sure your profile specifies your location. France Travail (formerly Pôle Emploi) — the French national job centre. Register here as soon as possible if you're job seeking — you may be entitled to unemployment benefits even as a foreigner. Indeed.fr and Welcome to the Jungle — strong for private sector and startup roles. EPSO — epso.europa.eu ↗ for EU institution competitions and contract agent roles. Council of Europe — coe.int/jobs ↗ for all CoE roles including internships. University of Strasbourg — unistra.fr ↗ for academic and administrative positions. On networking in Strasbourg: The city is smaller than it looks on paper. Everyone in the international community seems to know everyone else within about two degrees of separation. One good contact at the Council of Europe leads to another at the Parliament leads to an introduction at a startup. Go to events. Join the expat groups. Say yes to the after-work drinks. Your next job in this city will almost certainly come through someone you meet, not a job board. French work contracts explained French employment law is detailed and strongly protective of employees. Here are the contract types you'll encounter: Contract What it is Notice period For you CDI Contrat à Durée Indéterminée Permanent, open-ended contract. The gold standard in France — landlords, banks and CAF all love it. 1–3 months depending on seniority Aim for this. It unlocks everything. CDD Contrat à Durée Déterminée Fixed-term contract. Common for project-based work and replacements. Must have a legal reason to exist. End of contract date Fine for a start. Harder for renting. Intérim Temporary agency work. Flexible but no job security. Common in logistics and manufacturing. Very short or none Bridge option only. Freelance / Auto-entrepreneur Self-employment status. Simple to register, but you handle your own social contributions. None Popular in tech, translation, consulting. Working rights and key facts Working week: France has a 35-hour legal working week. In practice many professionals work more, but overtime rules apply. Minimum wage (SMIC): Around €1,800/month gross in 2026. Updated annually in January. Paid leave: 5 weeks (25 days) per year is the legal minimum. Many employers offer more. Payslip (fiche de paie): French payslips are dense — you'll see significant deductions for social contributions (healthcare, pension, unemployment). Your net salary is typically 75–78% of your gross. Lunch vouchers (tickets restaurant): Many employers provide these — subsidised meal vouchers worth €9–13/day. Accepted at most restaurants and supermarkets. Transport reimbursement: Employers are legally required to reimburse 50% of your public transport subscription (your CTS monthly pass). Alsace-Moselle social regime: Strasbourg falls under a special historical regime that gives employees additional benefits — including a supplementary day of paid leave and better sick pay coverage than the rest of France. One of the hidden perks of working here. ⚡ Alsace-Moselle bonus The Alsace-Moselle local regime is a legacy of the German period and gives workers here better social protection than anywhere else in France — higher sick pay reimbursement, an extra public holiday (Good Friday and St Stephen's Day on 26 December are both public holidays here but not in the rest of France), and a supplementary pension scheme. Worth knowing about — and worth mentioning in salary negotiations. Working across the border in Germany Strasbourg's proximity to Germany opens up a genuinely unusual professional option: you can live in France and work in Germany (or vice versa) as a travailleur frontalier (cross-border worker). The Ortenau region of Baden-Württemberg — Kehl, Offenburg, Freiburg — is commuting distance. German salaries in many sectors (tech, engineering, finance) are higher than French equivalents As a cross-border worker you pay taxes in the country where you work, but remain socially insured in your country of residence You need a carte de frontalier — a cross-border worker declaration — available from your local tax office The tram to Kehl takes 15 minutes. The train to Offenburg takes 20. Freiburg is 45 minutes by direct train. The commute question: Yes, some people genuinely commute from a Strasbourg flat to an office in Freiburg. Yes, it sounds unusual. Yes, they do it because the salary differential and the quality of life in Strasbourg make the maths work comfortably. Europe is strange and wonderful. Partners of institution staff — the titre de séjour situation If you work at the Council of Europe, the European Court of Human Rights, or another international organisation in Strasbourg, and your partner is a non-EU citizen, there is an important administrative reality to understand before you arrive — and one that is rarely explained clearly in onboarding documents. What happens to non-EU partners Non-EU spouses or partners of international organisation staff are issued a specific titre de séjour (residence permit) that grants them the right to live in France — but not automatically the right to work. This is different from the situation of spouses of EU citizens, who have automatic work rights under EU freedom of movement law. In practice, this means: Your partner can live legally in France and is covered by the French healthcare system Their residence permit will typically be stamped with a restriction — often worded as "ne confère pas le droit d'exercer une activité professionnelle" (does not confer the right to exercise a professional activity) If they want to work in France, a separate authorisation is required How to get a work authorisation as a non-EU partner This is the part that surprises most people: the work authorisation process for non-EU partners of institution staff does not go through the standard French immigration system. Instead, it goes through the institution where the staff member works. The staff member's HR or legal department submits a request to the French authorities on behalf of the partner The institution's status under its headquarters agreement with France gives it a specific role in validating the partner's right to work Processing times vary — expect several weeks to a few months Once approved, the partner receives an updated titre de séjour or a separate work authorisation document ⚡ Do this immediately on arrival As soon as you arrive in Strasbourg, the staff member should contact their institution's HR department and ask specifically about the process for their partner's work authorisation. Don't wait until your partner has a job offer — the process takes time and you want it running in the background from day one. The Council of Europe and the European Court of Human Rights both have specific protocols for this. Ask HR for the Protocol on Privileges and Immunities procedures relevant to family members — this is the legal framework that governs it. The honest reality: This situation — living legally but unable to work without a specific process — can be genuinely frustrating for partners who are highly qualified professionals. The paperwork is real, the waiting is real, and the limitation on spontaneously accepting work is real. The good news is that once the authorisation is in place, your partner can work normally. The bad news is that nobody tells you about this until you're already here. Now you know. 📚 Moving with children?
