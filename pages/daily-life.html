Daily Life in Strasbourg

Getting around, doing your shopping, eating well, and knowing which events to put in your calendar. Strasbourg rewards those who know how to navigate it — and this page is your cheat sheet.

Strasbourg is one of those cities that gets better the longer you live in it. The tram is genuinely excellent. Germany is 15 minutes away by tram. The food market on Saturday morning at Place du Marché is one of life's simple pleasures. And the Christmas market will ruin every other Christmas market you will ever attend for the rest of your life. Let's get into it.

Getting around — trams, bikes and buses

Strasbourg has one of the best urban tram networks in France. Clean, punctual, above ground (so no descending into dark tunnels), and the rails run through grass in several sections — which sounds like a minor detail until you live here and realise how much nicer it makes the city look.

The network is operated by CTS (Compagnie des Transports Strasbourgeois). Everything — trams, buses, and the cross-border line to Germany — runs on the same ticket system.

⚡ Pro tip

The Place de l'Homme de Fer is the central hub where most tram lines converge. Learn which lines go where from there and navigation becomes simple. Lines A, B, C, D, E and F all pass through or near it.

If you work at the European institutions in the Robertsau district, tram line E goes directly there. If you live in Neudorf, line A is your friend. If you're heading to Kehl, it's always tram D from Homme de Fer.

On tram etiquette: Strasbourgeois are serious about validating their tickets. You will see inspectors (contrôleurs) regularly — they work in plain clothes and reveal themselves in groups. The fine for not validating is €80. Validate every single time, even if you've been on the tram for one stop. You have been warned.

Opening hours — what's open, when, and the lunch break

One of the most disorienting things about arriving in France is discovering that the country operates on a very different schedule from what most newcomers expect. Understanding this early saves a lot of frustration.

The general rule

Day Shops & services Restaurants Markets
Monday – Friday 9am – 7pm (some until 7:30pm) Noon–2pm & 7pm–10pm —
Saturday 9am – 7pm Noon–2pm & 7pm–10pm 7am – 1pm
Sunday Mostly closed. Supermarkets until noon–1pm max. Some boulangeries open until 1pm. Some open, many closed — check ahead 7am – 1pm (Place du Marché, Place Broglie)
⚠️ Sunday in France is not a shopping day. It is a day of rest — legally protected and culturally sacred. Supermarkets that are open close at noon or 1pm at the very latest. Most shops, pharmacies (except the rotating pharmacie de garde), banks, post offices and administrative offices are completely closed. Do your weekly shop on Saturday morning. If you forget something on Sunday afternoon, you will be surviving on whatever is already in your fridge and the good grace of your neighbours.

The French lunch break — and how seriously they take it

France invented the lunch break. Or at least, France has perfected it into something approaching a cultural institution. Between approximately noon and 2pm, much of the country pauses. Not everywhere, not everyone — but enough that it will catch you out if you’re not prepared.

In practical terms, this means:

  • Restaurants serve lunch from noon to 2pm. Arrive at 2:15pm and the kitchen is closed. This is not negotiable, not flexible, and not subject to appeal. The chef has already started cleaning down. Plan accordingly.
  • Small shops and bakeries often close for lunch. Particularly independent shops, butchers, and specialist food stores. They reopen at 2pm or 2:30pm.
  • Administrative offices, banks and the préfecture typically close from noon to 1:30pm or 2pm. If you have an appointment at a government office, confirm the opening hours before you go — arriving at 12:30pm to find a locked door is a Strasbourg rite of passage.
  • Phone calls go unanswered. Calling a French company between noon and 2pm is an exercise in patience. The voicemail will pick up. They will call back at 2pm. Or possibly not until tomorrow.
  • The lunch break at the EU institutions is slightly more international in rhythm, but even there, the cafeterias fill up at noon and the building noticeably quiets between 1pm and 2pm.
A true story that every expat recognises: You need to go to the préfecture to hand in a document. You leave work at 12:45pm, walk twenty minutes there, and arrive to find the doors closed and a sign that says the office reopens at 2pm. You wait. At 2:01pm the doors open and there are already fifteen people in the queue ahead of you who apparently materialised from nowhere. You go back to work at 3:30pm having submitted one piece of paper. This is France. This is also, eventually, something you will find charming rather than maddening. It takes about a year.
⚡ Practical tips

If you need to run errands or make calls to French companies, aim for 10am–noon or 2:30pm–5pm. These are the golden windows when things actually happen.

For restaurants specifically — if you want lunch, arrive before 12:30pm at a popular spot or you may not get a table. The French take their lunch seriously enough that the good places fill up fast.

One silver lining: the lunch break culture means that proper sit-down lunches with colleagues are normal and expected, not something to feel guilty about. A one-hour lunch with a glass of wine is not decadence here — it’s Tuesday.

Commuting to Germany and shopping in Kehl

One of the genuinely wonderful things about living in Strasbourg is that Germany is right there. The town of Kehl, in the Baden-Württemberg region, is a 15-minute tram ride across the Rhine. You will go there more than you expect.

Getting to Kehl

Take Tram D from anywhere on the D line (it passes through Homme de Fer) in the direction of Kehl Rathaus. The tram crosses the Passerelle des Deux Rives — a striking footbridge over the Rhine — and you're in Germany. The journey from central Strasbourg takes about 15–20 minutes. Your standard CTS ticket or monthly pass is valid all the way to Kehl Rathaus.

You can also walk or cycle across the Passerelle in about 15 minutes — it's a pleasant walk with views of the Rhine in both directions.

⚠️ Border checks: France has periodically reintroduced temporary border checks at the Strasbourg-Kehl crossing since 2015. In practice this means occasional ID checks on the tram or at the bridge. Always carry your passport or EU ID card when crossing — not because it's legally required every time, but because you don't want to be the one person caught without it on the day they're checking.

⚡ Practical tips for Kehl

German shops accept euros but may prefer to give change in euros — no issue there. Most shops now accept card payments but smaller places may be cash-only. ATMs in Kehl dispense euros.

German shops are generally open Monday to Saturday until 8pm or later — significantly later than French shops. This makes a Kehl run on a weekday evening a genuinely useful option when you've run out of something and French shops are already closed.

The Kehl experience: You will go to Kehl for the first time thinking "I'll just grab some German bread and a few things from dm." You will return two hours later having spent €80, carrying four bags, and having eaten a döner kebab the size of your forearm. This happens to everyone. Budget accordingly.

Where to eat — Alsatian classics and beyond

Alsatian cuisine is one of Europe's great regional cooking traditions — heavily influenced by both French and German cultures, with a character entirely its own. Living in Strasbourg means you have access to it every day, which is one of the better perks of the job.

Alsatian dishes to know

  • Tarte flambée (Flammekueche) — the essential Alsatian dish. Thin crispy base, crème fraîche, onions and lardons. Eaten by the round, not by the slice. Order more than you think you need.
  • Choucroute garnie — sauerkraut braised with pork, sausages and potatoes. A serious meal for serious hunger. Best in winter.
  • Baeckeoffe — a slow-cooked casserole of three meats and vegetables in Riesling. Needs to be ordered 24 hours in advance at most restaurants — plan ahead.
  • Spätzle — soft egg noodles, often served with cheese. The Alsatian answer to pasta and just as comforting.
  • Munster cheese — pungent, strong, and deeply satisfying with a glass of Gewurztraminer. Your flat will smell for days. Worth it.
  • Bredeles — small Christmas biscuits, ubiquitous from November. You will eat an inadvisable quantity of them.

⚡ Dining tip

French restaurants serve lunch from noon to 2pm and dinner from 7pm to 10pm. Arriving at 2:30pm and expecting to be fed is a reliable way to be turned away with a polite but firm shake of the head. The kitchen is closed. This is not negotiable. Plan your meals accordingly — you will adapt faster than you think.

Yearly events every resident should know

Strasbourg has a rich calendar of annual events — some intimate and neighbourhood-level, some that draw millions of visitors. As a resident, knowing when these happen is useful both for planning your own life and for warning any guests who might visit.

June 21
Fête de la Musique
On the summer solstice, music fills every square, courtyard and street corner in Strasbourg — free, spontaneous and genuinely joyful. Everything from jazz quartets to amateur rock bands. The entire city is outside until late. One of the best nights of the year to live here.
July 13–14
Fête Nationale (Bastille Day) & Bal des Pompiers
France's national day is celebrated with a fireworks display over the Ill river on the evening of the 14th. But the real insider event is the Bal des Pompiers on the night of the 13th — the firefighters of the Caserne de Cronenbourg open their fire station to 4,000 people for a dance party. Sounds eccentric. Is actually one of the most fun events of the summer. Entry is free or very cheap. Get there early.
September
Journées du Patrimoine (Heritage Days)
One weekend per year, buildings normally closed to the public open their doors — the European Parliament, historic private residences, government buildings. A fascinating glimpse into spaces you'd otherwise never see. Entirely free.
September
Strasbourg Marathon
The city marathon runs through the historic centre and across into Germany. Even if you're not running, the city centre is transformed for the weekend — worth watching from a café terrace with a coffee.
October
Harvest festivals & wine routes
October is vendange (harvest) season in Alsace. The wine villages along the Route des Vins — Obernai, Ribeauvillé, Riquewihr — hold harvest festivals with tastings, music and local food. Absolutely worth a day trip. Rent a car or join an organised excursion.
Late Nov – Dec 24
Marché de Noël (Christmas Market)
Strasbourg's Christmas market is the oldest in France, dating to 1570, and consistently ranked among the best in Europe. The city is transformed: over 300 chalets spread across Place Kléber, Place Broglie, the cathedral square and beyond, selling mulled wine (vin chaud), bredeles, ornaments and gifts. Around two million visitors come each year — which means the city centre is very busy on weekends. As a resident, the secret is to go on a weekday evening when the crowds thin and the atmosphere becomes genuinely magical.
1st Wed / month
National siren test
Every first Wednesday of the month at noon, sirens sound across all of France for about 90 seconds as a national civil alert test. Des sirènes retentissent partout en France chaque premier mercredi du mois. Do not panic. You live here now. Nod, check your phone, continue with your day.
On the Christmas market: You will tell yourself you won't become one of those people who talks about the Strasbourg Christmas market as a defining life experience. You will then go on the first cold evening of November, drink one vin chaud under the illuminated cathedral, and immediately begin telling everyone you know to visit. This transformation takes approximately 20 minutes. There is no known cure.
