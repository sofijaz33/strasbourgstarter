Community in Strasbourg

Getting the admin sorted is one thing. Building an actual life — friends, routines, a sense of belonging — takes a little longer. Here's where to start.

Strasbourg is a genuinely welcoming city for internationals — helped enormously by the fact that roughly a quarter of its population is foreign-born, and that the EU institutions and University of Strasbourg create a constant flow of newcomers who are all in the same situation: curious, a little disoriented, and looking to meet people.

The city is small enough that a few good connections open up a whole social world, and large enough that you can find your tribe — whether that's language learners, runners, jazz musicians, parents, or people who just want to eat well and talk about it.

Expat groups and online communities

The fastest way to meet people in the same situation as you is through the existing expat groups — both online and in person. Strasbourg's international community is well-organised and welcoming to newcomers.

Facebook group
Strasbourg Expats
The main English-language expat group for Strasbourg. Questions, tips, recommendations, flat-sharing, events. Very active and genuinely helpful. The first place to post when you arrive.
facebook.com/groups/expats.strasbourg ↗
Meetup group
Strasbourg Internationals
Language exchange meetups every Tuesday and Friday at 8:30pm. 15–30 people from all backgrounds meet to practise languages, share cultures and make connections. Running since 2010. One of the best recurring events in the city.
meetup.com/strasbourg-international ↗
Meetup group
Strasbourg International Community
Social events, outings, drinks and cultural activities for internationals living in Strasbourg. More social than language-focused. Good for people who want to go out rather than sit down and study.
meetup.com/strasbourg-international-community ↗
Institution networks
Council of Europe & EP staff networks
Both the Council of Europe and the European Parliament have internal staff associations and social clubs — sports, culture, language courses, family events. If you work at either institution, explore these from day one. They're underused by newcomers.
⚡ First week tip

Join the Strasbourg Expats Facebook group before you arrive. Post an introduction message — "Just moved from X, working at Y, looking to meet people" — and you will have responses within hours. The community is genuinely warm and people remember being new themselves.

Language exchanges

Strasbourg is one of the best cities in Europe for language exchange — it sits at the intersection of French, German and English, with a large student and international population that makes multilingual conversations the norm rather than the exception.

Language exchange
Strasbourg Internationals meetups
Every Tuesday and Friday at 8:30pm at Au Coin des Craquelins (65 rue de Zurich). Informal, friendly, bar setting. Organisers wear hats and make introductions. Discount drinks for attendees. The easiest entry point into the international social scene.
Language exchange
Tandem language partners
One-on-one language exchange — you teach your language, they teach theirs. The University of Strasbourg runs a tandem programme, and Tandem partners are also easy to find via the Facebook group or Tandem app.
Language café
Café des Langues
Informal language café events in various bars around Strasbourg. Different language combinations each week. Check local Facebook groups and Meetup for current schedule — the format changes organisers occasionally.
On speaking French: Everyone in the expat community will tell you to learn French. They are right. Even basic French dramatically changes your relationship with the city — neighbours, shopkeepers, colleagues, the person at the préfecture who holds your residence permit in their hands. You don't need to be fluent. You need to try. Strasbourgeois respond warmly to effort, even imperfect effort. Show up with a few sentences and good will, and most interactions go better than you expect.

Sports clubs and outdoor life

Strasbourg is an excellent city for active people — flat terrain, 600km of cycle paths, the Rhine nearby, the Vosges mountains an hour away, and a culture that takes sport and outdoor life seriously.

Cycling

The most important sport in Strasbourg is arguably commuting. But if you want to ride for pleasure, the Rhine cycle path (EuroVélo 15) is one of Europe's great long-distance routes and runs right through the city. The Robertsau forest has off-road trails. Vélhop (the city bike scheme) is perfect for casual riding — €25/year for unlimited 30-minute trips.

Running

The Parc de l'Orangerie (3.5km loop), the Rhine banks, and the Robertsau forest are the main running spots. The Strasbourg Marathon in September attracts thousands of runners and is a great goal to work towards if you're building a routine. Several running clubs welcome newcomers — ask in the Strasbourg Expats group for current recommendations.

Football

Going to watch RC Strasbourg Alsace at La Meinau stadium is one of the great social rituals of living in this city. Tickets are accessible, the atmosphere is genuinely passionate, and it's one of the fastest ways to feel like a real resident rather than a visitor. Blue and white. Always.

Other sports

  • Swimming — Strasbourg has several municipal pools. The Piscine de la Kibitzenau is the most popular with the international community.
  • Tennis, padel, squash — multiple clubs across the city, the Council of Europe has a squash court on-site.
  • Hiking — the Vosges mountains start about 40 minutes west of the city. The GR5 long-distance trail passes through. Weekend hikes are a major part of Alsatian culture.
  • Skiing — La Schlucht and other Vosges resorts are 1–1.5 hours away. Not the Alps, but excellent for a quick day on the slopes.
  • Rowing and water sports — the Ill and the Rhine canals offer kayaking, rowing and paddleboarding. Several clubs are active and welcoming.

Culture, arts and volunteering

Strasbourg has a cultural life that punches well above its size — a function of being both a university city and a European capital.

  • Opéra National du Rhin — one of France's major opera houses, with productions in French, German, Italian and beyond. operanationaldurhin.eu ↗
  • Orchestre Philharmonique de Strasbourg — resident orchestra with a strong international reputation. Student and young professional discount tickets available.
  • Musée d'Art Moderne et Contemporain (MAMCS) — excellent contemporary art museum on the Ill. Free on the first Sunday of every month.
  • Musée Alsacien — traditional Alsatian culture, crafts and history. Wonderful for understanding where you actually are.
  • Festival Musica — international contemporary music festival every September. Genuinely adventurous programming.
  • Volunteering — the City of Strasbourg's youth bureau coordinates intercultural volunteer programmes. Several NGOs and associations also recruit English-speaking volunteers — ask in the expat Facebook group.

Learning French in Strasbourg

You will need some French. Here are the most practical options:

Language school
ILFRA / Alliance Française
Alliance Française Strasbourg offers structured French courses at all levels. Well-organised, good teachers, flexible schedules. Worth it if you want to make systematic progress rather than picking it up haphazardly.
alliancefrancaise.fr ↗
University
University of Strasbourg — French courses
The University offers French language courses for non-native speakers at various levels. More affordable than private schools, and you meet other international students. Check their international student services page.
unistra.fr ↗
App + exchange
Self-study + conversation practice
Duolingo or Pimsleur for structure, language exchange meetups for speaking practice. The combination is remarkably effective if you're consistent. Most people reach functional French within 6–9 months of living here.
⚡ The fastest way to improve your French

Turn off the English subtitles on French TV. Order in French even when the waiter offers you English. Make one French-speaking friend and insist on speaking French with them even when it's painful. The discomfort is temporary. The progress is permanent.

Also: Alsatians are patient with non-native French speakers. More so than Parisians. You are in the right city to learn.

Honest tips for making friends in Strasbourg

Making friends as an adult in a new city is genuinely hard. Here's what actually works in Strasbourg, based on collective expat experience:

  • Say yes to the first invitation. Even if you're tired, even if you don't feel like it. The first social event is always slightly awkward. The second is much easier. The third is when you start to feel settled.
  • The language exchange meetups are magic. Strasbourg Internationals on Tuesday and Friday evenings. Go once and you will understand why it has been running since 2010.
  • The institution onboarding events are worth attending. If you work at the Council of Europe or the Parliament, go to the welcome events even if they seem corporate. Everyone there is new. Everyone is looking to meet people.
  • School pickup is underrated. If you have children, the school gate is one of the fastest ways into the local social fabric. The parents of your children's classmates become your social network faster than almost anything else.
  • Join something with a regular schedule. Running club, language class, choir, rowing — anything that puts you in the same place with the same people every week. Regularity builds friendship more reliably than single events.
  • Be patient with yourself. The first three months are often the hardest. The city makes more sense at six months. By one year, you'll be the one telling newcomers where to go.
The honest truth: Strasbourg is a city that rewards patience. It is not immediately obvious or easily legible the way some cities are. But it reveals itself gradually — through the Saturday market, through a good meal in a winstub, through a warm evening on the Krutenau quays, through the moment you notice you are navigating the tram without thinking. Give it time. It will click. And when it does, you will understand why people who were only supposed to stay for two years are still here at ten.
